Why two New Hanover roofing quotes can differ by 40%

Montgomery County’s roofing market is saturated, especially as you get closer to the Philadelphia metro, and New Hanover sits in a cluster of owner-occupied homes where contractors compete directly. Quotes for architectural shingle replacement here can range from $11,000–$23,000, and the difference is rarely random— it comes down to quantifiable factors tied to local houses and weather.

Many homes in New Hanover are in subdivisions built after World War II with similar construction methods and materials, but the condition and previous reroofing history of each house varies. Some roofs have two or even three layers of old shingles, especially if they haven’t been fully stripped before. Each additional layer adds landfill costs—figure $500–$1,500 per layer for tear-off and disposal alone. Decking problems hidden until tear-off can add $75–$150 per plywood sheet replaced, and this is more common in homes with subtle leaks after decades of use.

Climate is another concrete price driver. With a 30 psf ground snow load and typical frost depths of 30 inches, plus a moderate freeze-thaw cycle, New Hanover roofs need enough ice barrier at eaves and better ventilation than is required south or west of here. Installation labor rises if the pitch is steep—this part of Montgomery County sees plenty of 6/12 to 9/12 slopes, which slow the job and require proper harnesses, tacking on 20-30% to labor costs. Material grade also matters: upgraded Class 4 impact-resistant shingles bump a job from $18,000–$23,000 for the same roof footprint.

Reading a roofing estimate line by line

Reading a New Hanover roofing estimate starts with the “square” count: every quote must say how many roofing squares—units of 100 square feet—you’re paying to replace, along with the pitch. If you don’t see these numbers in black and white, you can’t compare bids or check how much material is actually being installed. Tear-off and disposal should be itemized, listing the number of layers to remove and specifying if a dumpster is included.

Look for underlayment and ice barrier types spelled out by brand or spec (“synthetic underlayment to all slopes,” “ice and water shield 3 feet up from eaves”), not just a generic “felt.” Decking should have a per-sheet contingency price in case rotten sections need replacing. Flashings, pipe boots, ridge vent—these are minor individually but failing to list them usually means they’re skimped or left for afterthoughts.

Warranty terms must distinguish shingle or material warranty from labor/workmanship coverage. Payment schedule is another critical line: in New Hanover, paying more than 15% up front is risky; the balance should fall on completion. A vague lump sum with no measurement is worth less than the paper it’s on.

What New Hanover's housing stock means for your roof

Most roofs in New Hanover were installed on homes built during the same postwar tract housing wave that reshaped Abington and Cheltenham—long before modern codes for ice barriers, attic ventilation or OSB decking. With 32.2% of local homes built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), replacing a roof often involves working over old plank or early plywood decking, which is more likely to show rot or nail pops. These older structures tend to have steeper roof pitches, a design to shed snow before modern roof ventilation and underlayment standards came into practice.

For homes built in the late 1990s or early 2000s, which are just now reaching their first replacement roofs, decking is usually plywood or OSB and may show fewer moisture issues, but water stains and delams from 25+ years of Pennsylvania freeze-thaw cycles are not rare. Wherever you are in New Hanover, it’s uncommon to find more than two layers up there (especially since most area municipalities enforced full tear-offs by the late 1980s), but you do sometimes run into “ghost” second layers on houses that missed a code cycle.

In practical terms: expect older roofs to need at least some decking patch, especially along eaves. If your house never had an ice and water barrier, appending it at replacement will help stave off the leaks that plague many neighboring homes after one wild thaw or windstorm.

Permits and code requirements in New Hanover

Roofing work in New Hanover falls under the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), which is based on the IRC and enforced by local municipalities unless they’ve opted out. In Montgomery County, different townships—including those bordering New Hanover—run their own code enforcement offices. As in Norristown, replacing more than 25% of an existing roof surface generally requires a building permit; routine shingle repairs covering less than a square usually do not in most area townships.

Contractors taking on roofing work valued over $500 must hold a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration, issued by the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General. You can and should verify this registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov before signing anything. For jobs involving employees, Pennsylvania requires workers’ compensation insurance; homeowners can confirm current coverage using WCAIS at wcais.pa.gov. These checks protect you from scams and liability—do not skip them even if the contractor is well recommended.

Finally, federal law applies if your home predates 1978: contractors must comply with EPA Lead RRP Rule, and you can verify renovator credentials at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov. There are no trade licenses for roofing itself in the state—registration and insurance are what matter for consumer protection.

Decking, ventilation and flashing — the hidden roof costs

Most major cost surprises in New Hanover roofing don’t come from the visible shingles but from what’s uncovered once those shingles come off. Decking repairs are a prime culprit—on homes built before plywood became standard, you could need multiple sheets replaced, at $75–$150 each for labor, materials and landfill. Even newer homes develop edge rot near gutters and chimneys, where leaks go unnoticed for years.

Ventilation is another pocketbook hit. Southeastern Pennsylvania's freeze-thaw cycles and humid summers make poor attic airflow a recipe for both winter ice damming and summer shingle curling. Insufficient or blocked soffit and ridge venting can also void manufacturer warranties. If upgrades are needed, expect a range of $350–$750 for full ridge vent retrofit on an average roof.

Flashing and leak-prone details—chimneys, step flashing on dormers, rubber vent boots, drip edges—require careful attention. Replacing all flashings and boots will add $400–$1,100 to most roof jobs, but skipping them is more costly if wind-driven rain works behind your new shingles. Most flashing and ventilation problems in New Hanover homes weren’t “extras” on the original roofs—they just weren’t standard yet. Today, they’re the difference between a leak-free 25-year system and an early warranty claim.

Asphalt, metal or membrane — what suits a New Hanover roof

In southeastern Pennsylvania’s climate—where a 30 psf ground snow load and a 14°F winter design temperature are your baseline—the choice of roofing material really comes down to how your home handles snow, wind and freeze-thaw. Asphalt architectural shingles dominate in New Hanover and the wider Montgomery County because they balance cost and durability for the pitch and size of homes here, and townships like Upper Dublin often require them for HOA uniformity. With a replacement range of $11,000–$23,000, they remain the default.

Metal standing seam roofs perform best for snow shedding and longevity in this region—they shed Montco's wet snow loads while shrinking ice dam risk—but the installed price of $26,500–$47,000 puts them out of reach for many replacement budgets, especially since they rarely deliver full resale value unless the house is higher-end or exposed. Their biggest edge is over steep-pitched homes with tricky valleys or heavy tree cover.

Flat roofs, porches, and rear additions call for TPO or EPDM membrane, with a price spectrum of $14,000–$27,500: right for low slope, not for main house fields. Impact-resistant (Class 4) architectural shingles bring incremental improvement for wind-driven storms or scattered hail, running $18,000–$23,000. Unless you get a strong insurance discount, most New Hanover roofs get more value from siding or gutter upgrades for the same dollar.

Frequently asked questions — roofing in New Hanover

Can I install new shingles over old ones in Pennsylvania? In Pennsylvania, including New Hanover and other Montgomery County communities, you are sometimes allowed to install a new asphalt shingle roof over an old one—but that doesn’t mean you should. State code, enforced locally, lets you have up to two layers of roofing, so if your home only has one layer, your roofer might suggest going over the top. Practically, though, most homes in New Hanover with any age on them have subtle deck damage or ventilation problems that go undiagnosed unless the old shingles are removed. Ice and water barrier, now critical in our freeze-thaw climate, is impossible to install without a full tear-off. And the price difference isn’t as much as you might think: a full tear-off typically adds just $500–$1,500 to a job in the $11,000–$23,000 range, but skipping it can shorten your new roof’s life by years. For pre-1980 homes especially, full removal is almost always worth it.
Why is my New Hanover roofing quote higher than the online average? Local roofing costs in New Hanover, which run $11,000–$23,000 for an architectural shingle replacement, may seem high compared to online averages or national "DIY estimator" calculators. There are a few concrete reasons for this. First, New Hanover has larger-than-average roof footprints due to the high percentage of single-family home stock (over 91%), which directly impacts material and labor. Second, permit and code enforcement standards in Montgomery County push professional contractors to do the job right—full tear-offs for two-layer roofs, mandatory ice barrier upgrades, and proper disposal—all of which are not required in many lower-regulation regions. Third, labor rates and insurance requirements (such as mandatory workers’ compensation) are higher than in rural Pennsylvania. Financially, a cheaper quote often means corners are being cut.
Will the crew damage my landscaping or driveway? On a standard New Hanover roof replacement, reputable crews take specific measures to protect your landscaping and driveway. Most jobs involve a dumpster parked for tear-off disposal. Crews will usually lay out plywood to prevent damage from falling debris and wheels, and tarps to collect nails and shingle scraps. Still, it’s important to clarify this in writing: have your estimate specify driveway or plant protection. Minor flattening of shrubs or grass along the roofline is possible, and old asphalt driveways may get new scrapes if the dumpster is shifted. If your bid comes in at the lower end ($11,000–$14,000 range), check whether site protection is included, as low bids sometimes skip this step. Ask before signing—the best defense is clarity up front.
Is a metal roof worth the extra cost in New Hanover? Metal roofing costs in New Hanover—often $26,500–$47,000—are about twice to three times the price of architectural shingles, so it’s a real investment. For most homes, especially the postwar single-family houses typical of our area, you’ll see the biggest value if your roof is steep, you have shade issues (to reduce moss and tree debris problems), or you plan to stay for multiple decades. Metal sheds snow and ice better than any other material and laughs at local windstorms, nearly eliminating the ice dam problems older homes face. That said, most won’t recover the entire cost at resale unless the house is high-end or architecturally distinctive, and HOA rules may restrict your options. For the classic $300,000–$600,000 New Hanover home, architectural shingles are usually the sweet spot for return on investment.
Should I use a local roofer or a national company? In New Hanover, where roofing jobs draw from a dense Montgomery County contractor pool, there’s no built-in advantage to going with a national franchise. Local companies are tightly regulated: HIC registration is mandatory for jobs over $500, and national names usually subcontract the job back out to local crews anyway. A local roofer relies on regional reputation—within 10 miles, word travels—and is more likely to understand nuances like code enforcement, neighborhood HOA rules, and historical storm damage in the Schuylkill valley. Either way, check the contractor’s registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Expect similar price bands regardless: $11,000–$23,000 for an architectural shingle roof.
Do metal roofs shed snow better in New Hanover? Yes—metal standing seam roofs are superior for snow shedding in southeastern Pennsylvania’s climate, including New Hanover’s moderate but unpredictable winters. With a 30 psf ground snow load and the freeze-thaw patterns we see here, metal’s smooth surface and minimal seams let snow and ice slide off rapidly, reducing the risk of ice dams along your eaves. This has the added benefit of preventing backup leaks in houses with marginal insulation or older ventilation setups. The main downside is price: metal roofs run from $26,500–$47,000 versus $11,000–$23,000 for architectural shingles. Unless you have a persistent snow/ice issue or a high-value home, metal is best for owners who value longevity over first-cost.
Will filing a roof claim raise my premium? In Pennsylvania, including New Hanover, filing a claim for storm or wind damage on your roof can affect your homeowner’s insurance premium at renewal. While state law limits how a claim can be used, insurance companies track weather damage claims, and multiple claims in a few years are likely to result in higher rates—or even nonrenewal. For a smaller repair job in the $550–$2,200 range, it often makes sense to pay out of pocket to avoid a claim. For a complete reroof after a major storm or tree strike, a claim may be unavoidable, but consider your deductible and whether your insurer “surcharges” for weather incidents before you file. Always have damage documented by a reputable local contractor before contacting your carrier.
Why do roofs fail faster in southeastern Pennsylvania? Southeastern Pennsylvania, including New Hanover, puts roofs through the wringer: freeze-thaw cycles, humid summers, and the occasional tropical remnant. Fluctuating temperatures put stress on both shingles and fasteners, opening up nail pops and microcracks in older materials. Local homes built before 1980 often lack modern ice barrier and continuous ventilation, meaning that trapped attic moisture and minor eave leaks have years to do hidden damage. Even the best shingles replaced today in New Hanover last 25–30 years maximum—on the low end for the U.S.—because of our specific climate data: 14°F winter design temperature and 30 psf snow load. When you mix in Schuylkill valley windstorms, planned replacement in the $11,000–$23,000 range is a repeating local reality.
Is a sagging roof line an emergency? A sagging roof line on a New Hanover home almost always means trouble that shouldn’t wait. Here, most sags turn up in older houses with original plank decking, water damage from undetected ice damming, or heavy snow load stress near valleys or eaves. It’s not a “wait and see” situation: sagging can signal rot in the roof structure, failing rafters, or dangerous weight loading. Sometimes, a localized repair in the $550–$2,200 band can stop damage. Other times, a partial or full roof tear-off and framing repair is required, which could nudge total costs into the full replacement range. Have a reputable, registered local roofer and, if necessary, a structural carpenter inspect the problem before anyone gives you a price.
