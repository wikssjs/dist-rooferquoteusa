Checking out a roofer in St. Thomas: what to verify

Franklin County's thinner rural contractor market means St. Thomas homeowners need to be thorough when checking out a roofing company. Always start with the basics: under Pennsylvania law, any roofing contract over $500 requires the contractor to have a valid Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration, issued by the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General. You can verify that number directly at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For employer crews (not one-man bands), workers' compensation coverage is mandatory and can be checked via the Workers’ Compensation Automation and Integration System (WCAIS) at wcais.pa.gov. For homes built before 1978, make sure the firm is EPA Lead RRP certified—especially important in a region where more than 60% of homes are older, to protect you against dangerous dust. Remember, written contracts are required by law, along with a 3-day right to cancel.

Filing a roof claim after a Pennsylvania storm

After a hail or wind event in Franklin County, St. Thomas homeowners should thoroughly document the damage—take photos of both the roof and anything on the ground like fallen shingles before any cleanup. Your next step is to call your insurer to start a claim and carefully review the scope of loss the adjuster prepares. The first check you receive is often the actual cash value (ACV), which factors in depreciation. Once you show proof of completed work, you’ll get the balance up to the full replacement cost, based on your coverage.

Written contracts are required under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act for any job over $500, and you’re entitled to a 3-business-day right of cancellation. Never sign an agreement with a roofer that offers to “cover” your insurance deductible—waiving your deductible is illegal in Pennsylvania and violates consumer protection law. If a contractor suggests this, it’s a clear signal to walk away; reputable, registered contractors in St. Thomas know they must work transparently and within the law.

Insurers in this area are well aware of the wind exposure along the Cumberland Valley, and adjusters will especially scrutinize older homes built before 1980 for maintenance issues that might influence your claim. Don’t allow any roofer to start repairs or remove materials before you’ve documented conditions and a written scope is in hand. If damage is severe and the home is exposed, emergency tarping can generally be reimbursed, but save your receipts and still follow all insurer requirements in sequence.

Roofing permits in St. Thomas: what you need before work starts

Pennsylvania follows the Uniform Construction Code (UCC), which is based on the IRC and enforced locally. In Franklin County, code enforcement varies by municipality. For St. Thomas homeowners, if the work is limited to direct replacement of asphalt shingles (with no sheathing or framing repair), a building permit is generally not required in the unincorporated township. However, if your project involves replacing structural sheathing or making any changes to framing—especially common with older homes or after storm damage—the work triggers a building permit in municipalities like Chambersburg Borough, which handles its own enforcement through the Codes Enforcement Office.

For contractor verification, look up the Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Workers’ compensation is mandatory for employers, and coverage status can be checked through WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). There is no separate state roofing trade license in Pennsylvania; HIC registration is the required credential above $500. For pre-1978 homes, if lead paint is disturbed, the contractor must meet federal EPA Lead RRP requirements—you can verify this on certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov.

If a contractor can’t readily supply their HIC number, insurance papers, or workers’ comp proof, don’t proceed. The right paperwork follows state law and protects you legally and financially during the project.

The scope items St. Thomas homeowners don't budget for

In St. Thomas, the most common budget-busters on a roofing project are the “hidden” scope items that only show up after the first shingle comes off. With most local housing stock built before 1980, rot or damage to the wood decking is common. Deck replacement usually runs $85–$120 per 4x8 sheet, and it’s not unusual for older homes with past leaks to need five or more.

Attic ventilation is another issue many homeowners miss—especially critical here, where winter design temperatures hit 12 °F and freeze-thaw cycles are frequent. Insufficient ventilation traps moisture and can drive ice damming, which in this region often voids the manufacturer’s shingle warranty. Correcting venting can add $450–$900 for ridge vent and intake improvements.

Other overlooked scope items: chimney and wall flashing ($375–$1,200 depending on complexity); replacing old vent boots ($45–$150 each); drip edge at eaves/laps ($250–$600 for a whole roof); and ridge vents, which are increasingly mandated for warranty and performance reasons. Many low quotes conveniently omit these, resulting in a final invoice a couple thousand dollars higher than expected. Insist that your estimate explicitly lists pricing for these common extras, as they’re more likely than not on a typical St. Thomas roof.

Asphalt, metal or membrane — what suits a St. Thomas roof

In south-central Pennsylvania, a ground snow load of 30 psf and winter lows down to 12 °F mean the roof you pick matters a lot. Metal standing seam is valued for its longevity and ability to shed snow—no small feat here, where freeze-thaw cycles can drive shingle edges up and cause leaks at valleys. However, at $21,500–$38,000, it’s not the practical choice for every home, especially since the median household income in St. Thomas is $57,186 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024).

Architectural asphalt shingles remain dominant here, not just because they cost less ($8,800–$18,500) but because nearly all postwar subdivision roofs were stick-framed and originally specified for asphalt. Their 25–30 year lifespan suits the climate if attic ventilation is right and details like flashing are handled well. Class 4 impact-resistant shingles offer more protection on the most wind-exposed lots, especially along Cumberland Valley ridges, but come at the high end of the shingle range ($14,500–$18,500).

TPO and EPDM membranes are the only sensible choices for flat or low-slope roofs—architectural shingles simply won’t perform on a pitch below 3:12. Their cost ($11,000–$22,500) lines up with their specialized application and maintenance demands.

How to compare roofing quotes in St. Thomas

A solid roofing estimate in St. Thomas spells out details you need to compare offers confidently. Start with the roof area—listed by squares (100 square feet), with pitch specified, as this drives both labor and waste. Tear-off and disposal should be separated by number of layers, with dumpster or haul-away costs shown. Underlayment and ice barrier need to be named by product type (not just “felt”), and how much is being used. Decking replacement must be listed per sheet as a contingency—otherwise you risk a padded invoice later if rot appears.

Look for specific mentions of flashing, drip edge, ridge vent, and vent boot replacements. Warranties should be broken down so you see both workmanship (contractor’s labor) and manufacturer (shingle/material) coverage. Payment schedule matters: deposits should be small, and most of your money only changes hands after work is satisfactorily complete. If any estimate is missing details—lump sum with no measurement, or “materials/labor” as a single line—walk away. You can’t compare what isn’t specified.

Frequently asked questions — roofing in St. Thomas

What warranty should a St. Thomas roofer offer?

In St. Thomas, a roofing contractor should offer two types of warranty: a manufacturer warranty on the shingles or roofing materials, and a labor or workmanship warranty covering their installation. For architectural asphalt shingles, you should expect a limited lifetime manufacturer warranty (often pro-rated after the first decade) and a workmanship warranty of at least 5 years, ideally 10. This split matters: manufacturer coverage won’t pay for leaks if the roofer installed things improperly—only the labor warranty does. On premium roofs like metal standing seam (which costs $21,500–$38,000), reputable crews may guarantee labor for up to 20 years. Never accept a quote that gives you just the shingle warranty, and always receive all warranties in writing alongside your contract. In Pennsylvania, state law requires a written contract and you have a 3-day right of cancellation. Established Franklin County roofers will spell all this out clearly, and it’s worth confirming how claims are handled locally.

Does attic ventilation matter in St. Thomas's climate?

Yes, attic ventilation is critical in St. Thomas’s climate. Our winters see deep cold snaps and snow loads up to 30 psf, while spring brings major freeze-thaw cycling. Poor ventilation traps warm, moist air, which often melts snow on the roof unevenly and causes ice dams at the eaves—the kind of problem that leads directly to leaks even when your shingles are still within their service life. In fact, shingle manufacturers often void their warranties over persistent ventilation issues. If your last roof failed early or you see icicle buildup along the gutters, ventilation may be to blame. Budgeting $450–$900 for ridge vent or additional soffit intake can prevent costly damage down the road. Franklin County’s housing, with so many homes built before 1980, sees this problem more often than you’d think—and it’s money well spent here.

Does homeowner's insurance cover roof replacement in St. Thomas?

Homeowner’s insurance can cover roof replacement in St. Thomas, but only when damage is due to sudden and accidental events like wind, hail, or falling trees—not for normal aging or wear. Insurers in Pennsylvania look very carefully at weather patterns specific to our area, and adjusters often scrutinize roofs on older St. Thomas houses for pre-existing issues before paying out. Coverage is typically based on actual cash value (ACV) up front, with the rest paid once work is finished—so you’ll still need to cover your deductible and any depreciation if your policy doesn’t include full replacement cost. It’s crucial not to be tempted by any offer to “waive your deductible”—that’s illegal here, and a roof replacement in St. Thomas runs $8,800–$18,500 for shingles. Always document storm damage before cleanup and insist on a detailed scope of work from your roofer.

How much does a flat roof replacement cost in St. Thomas?

For flat or low-slope roofs in St. Thomas—common on additions, porches, or some older ranch-style homes—you’re looking at membrane systems like TPO or EPDM. The installed price for these in 2026 is usually between $11,000–$22,500, with a typical home landing close to $16,000. The cost is higher than architectural shingles because flat roofs require special materials to handle ponding water and can’t rely on gravity for drainage. If your current flat roof is leaking or the seams are failing after 20 years, replacement is the best bet—repairs rarely last at this age in our climate. Be wary of anyone quoting far outside these numbers or pushing a “roll roofing” quick fix. In south-central Pennsylvania, proper membrane installation is essential for lasting performance.

Is ice barrier underlayment required in St. Thomas?

While not every township in Franklin County mandates ice barrier underlayment, it’s strongly recommended for St. Thomas roofs. Our local winter design temperature is 12 °F, and snow buildup at eaves is common. For most shingle systems, two rows (about 6 feet) of ice and water shield are now standard along eaves—some insurance carriers and all shingle manufacturers expect it. This elastic underlayment seals around nails and helps prevent water back-ups from ice dams from reaching the decking. The cost for proper coverage ranges from $350–$700 for a standard house. Skipping this may save a few hundred dollars up front, but homeowners here know it’s asking for leaks and no warranty protection later. Always check that your contract specifies both the amount and location of ice barrier.

What happens if rotted decking is found after tear-off?

If roofers discover rotted or soft decking after removing the shingles—a common scenario in St. Thomas given how much local housing is 40+ years old—they’ll need your approval to replace it. A reputable crew will show you the damaged sections and price repairs by the sheet, usually at $85–$120 per 4x8 panel. Decking that’s bad enough to flex underfoot or shows deep water staining cannot be reused; putting new shingles on top would void warranties and invite further leaks. Make sure your initial contract specifies a per-sheet replacement price so you don’t get blindsided. Most of the “extra charges” we see in Franklin County jobs are from hidden rot—not upsells. If the quote doesn’t mention contingencies for decking, press for detail before you sign anything.

What are the signs of hail damage on a roof?

In St. Thomas, hail storms are less frequent than in some other areas but still a threat, especially on homes sitting openly on the valley floor or exposed ridges. Hail damage typically appears as distinct, round divots or bruises in your shingle granules. Severe hits may crack shingles or knock granules off entirely, exposing the asphalt and leading eventually to leaks. After a storm, look for granules in your gutters or downspouts—an early warning sign. Insurance claims for hail damage usually trigger a detailed inspection from both your roofer and an adjuster; a repair can sometimes be done for $475–$1,750 if isolated, but widespread impact means a full replacement. Always have a reputable local roofer inspect—out-of-town “hail chasers” are a risk in Franklin County after storms.

How much does it cost to repair a roof leak in St. Thomas?

Typical roof leak repairs in St. Thomas cost between $475–$1,750, with the average job around $1,100. Price depends on what’s causing the leak: replacing a vent boot or flashing usually sits at the low end, while larger repairs to valleys or fixing past installation errors can run higher. Keep in mind, many Franklin County homes have roofs from the 1960s–70s, and leaks often trace back to details that just never got fixed properly—like missing ice barrier or old, rusted flashing at chimneys. If the leak is on a flat roof or low slope, costs trend up slightly due to labor. Always ask for a written diagnosis and scope of repair, and confirm whether the quoted warranty covers that patched section or just materials.

Do metal roofs shed snow better in St. Thomas?

Absolutely, metal standing seam roofs shed snow far more efficiently than asphalt shingles in St. Thomas. This matters because our region sees moderate but steady snowfall, and the design temp hits 12 °F, creating perfect ice dam conditions on slow-melting roofs. Metal’s smooth surface and lack of granules mean snow slides off as soon as the sun comes out, lowering load on rafters—especially useful for older farmhouses with original framing. However, the upgrade is substantial, with installed prices at $21,500–$38,000. Unless you plan to own your home for decades or need to solve chronic snow and ice problems, a well-detailed architectural shingle roof with proper insulation and ventilation will serve 90% of homeowners well in this climate. Consider metal if snow sliding is a real hazard or your roof is especially vulnerable.
