Reading the warranty on a New London roof

When you invest in a new roof in New London, two warranties come into play, and it’s essential to understand the difference. The first is the manufacturer’s warranty, which covers the roofing material against defects. This is usually prorated—the older your shingles, the less coverage remains. It’s only valid if the roof is installed to manufacturer's specifications. For example, if the installer skipped necessary ventilation or put new shingles over old ones, your claim could be denied. Most manufacturers require you to register the warranty within a strict time frame after installation; miss this step and you may be out of luck.

The second is the workmanship warranty, which covers the quality of the installer's labor. Unlike the material warranty, this is only as good as the company’s reputation and longevity. A “25-year” workmanship warranty from a contractor who’s only been in business two years is not meaningful protection, especially in a saturated market like Chester County where companies tend to come and go. Always clarify if the workmanship warranty is transferable if you sell your house—many are not, which can affect your resale value in a high homeownership community like New London.

Finally, keep all documentation. Any missed paperwork, unresolved code violations, or improper attic ventilation can void your protection fast. Know exactly what you’re getting and who’s standing behind it before signing.

Decking, ventilation and flashing — the hidden roof costs

Many New London homeowners are caught off guard when the final invoice for their roof project climbs beyond the initial bid. The main culprits are hidden conditions—rot in the roof decking, poor attic ventilation, and deteriorated flashing or vent boots—that only show up once old material is removed. With the humid summers and frequent freeze-thaw cycles in Chester County, attic ventilation isn’t just about energy bills: without it, moisture gets trapped, plywood rots, and shingle warranties are voided due to heat and ice damming. Remediating poor attic ventilation can add $800–$2,100 to an asphalt shingle job, but skipping it means risking the whole investment.

Decking replacement is another wildcard. In subdivisions built from the 1970s–1990s, plenty of roof decks are now past their best years. If rot is found at tear-off, plan for $100–$140 per sheet of plywood required. Chimney and wall flashings—not just the visible metal, but the layers underneath—are failure points in older homes that must be inspected and often rebuilt, costing $350–$900 depending on complexity. Don't forget small items: new vent boots run $45–$95 each, and proper drip edge (required by code) is a minor but essential expense.

Ridge vent installation, if needed to bring older homes up to code, generally runs $550–$900 for a typical suburban roof. All these details should be discussed and priced up front—avoid contractors who shrug off “hidden” items or act like contingencies are a scam. In New London’s market, they’re simply reality.

What insurance covers on a New London roof

If a windstorm or falling limb damages your New London roof, knowing how to navigate the insurance process is vital. Start by taking thorough photos of any damage and affected interior areas—before anyone does cleanup or makes repairs. Once you contact your insurer, request a written "scope of loss" outlining what they'll pay to repair or replace. Insurance typically pays on an "actual cash value" basis first (meaning the cost to repair or replace, less depreciation), with the replacement cost—the full amount—paid out after you complete the work and submit proof.

An adjuster visit is standard in Chester County, where roof damage is frequent after Ridge-line wind events. Don’t let anyone talk you into jumping ahead of this process or “handling” the insurance paperwork for you. It’s illegal for contractors in Pennsylvania to offer to cover or waive your deductible—and an offer like that is a red flag for a scam or future insurance trouble. Contractors who promise “no out of pocket expense” are violating the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, and you should walk away. You’re entitled to a written contract—required by law for work over $500—and a three-business-day right to cancel.

Once you receive your insurer's decision, share it with your contractor to confirm their estimate lines up with what insurance will cover. Good roofers in New London are used to scrutinizing insurance scopes and making sure everything needed for full repair is included. Don't skip this review, especially when dealing with adjusters who may not know local permitting, code or HOAs in Chester County subdivisions.

Roofing permits in New London: what you need before work starts

In New London (Chester County), roofing projects must comply with the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), which is based on the IRC. Full roof replacement that involves structural deck repair typically requires a building permit if you live within West Chester Borough, as enforced by the West Chester Borough Codes Department. However, if you’re only repairing up to one roofing square of like-for-like shingles, no permit is generally needed in the borough. Surrounding townships, such as those near West Goshen and Uwchlan, run their own code offices and may have similar or slightly different requirements, but the UCC is the core standard countywide.

Before signing any contract, always verify that your contractor holds a valid Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General—use hic.attorneygeneral.gov to confirm. For any job involving employees, Pennsylvania law mandates that your contractor carries workers’ compensation insurance. You can check their insurance status directly through the state's WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov).

If your home was built before 1978, federal EPA Lead RRP Rule applies for any job disturbing painted surfaces—another point your roofer should address. In short, in Chester County, a reputable roofer won’t dodge permits or proper registration. Insist on documentation before work begins.

What a proper New London roofing quote contains

Reading a roofing quote for your New London home should not feel like a guessing game. Ask for the roof area to be stated in squares (a square is 100 sq ft) and have the slope or pitch included. Tear-off and disposal should be itemized—listing the number of layers to be removed and whether dumpster fees are included. Your quote should specify which underlayment and ice barrier products will be used, not just generic “felt,” and note the height (in feet or courses) for ice and water shield at the eaves.

Decking rot is common in homes built from the 1970s on, so your estimate needs a contingency line for replacing plywood, typically priced per sheet. Flashing replacement (at chimneys, walls, and penetrations) and vent boot replacement should also be spelled out. Pay special attention to warranty: installation workmanship should be separated from the shingle manufacturer’s coverage. Finally, the payment schedule should require no more than 15% upfront, with the balance on completion—large cash deposits before work starts are a warning sign in the busy Chester County market.

TPO and EPDM on New London homes

Flat or low-slope roofs are not just a city problem—many New London homes have a rear addition, porch or sunroom where the roof pitch is too shallow for shingles to work. On anything below a 3/12 pitch, shingles will trap water, create leaks, and fail rapidly in our climate. The right approach is a membrane roof: TPO and EPDM are the most common in Chester County for these sections. They’re built to resist ponding and are sealed tight at the seams, something shingles can’t do on a flat plane.

A good TPO or EPDM roof should last 15–25 years here, but only if installed with proper drainage and edge termination. Where ponding water is an issue, membrane warranties may not apply. Always ask your roofer about tapering, scuppers, or new drains if there’s standing water for days after a rain. If someone suggests using shingles on a 2/12 or flatter slope, that’s a red flag—they’re telling you price matters more than protecting your home. For most flat roof replacements on New London homes, expect to pay between $14,500–$29,500 depending on size, tear-off, and complexity.

Frequently asked questions — roofing in New London

Do metal roofs shed snow better in New London? Metal standing seam roofs are very effective at shedding snow, even during southeast Pennsylvania’s heavier winter events. In New London, where the ground snow load is 30 psf and the winter design temperature is 14 °F, metal roofs quickly release accumulated snow—reducing ice damming and the risk of backup leaks along eaves. This can be a particular advantage if your house is exposed to Ridge-line wind and snow drifting near the South Valley Hills. Metal systems cost more initially—typically $28,000–$50,000 installed—but their snow-shedding ability and long lifespan (often 40–70 years) make them a reliable option for homeowners who want less winter maintenance and fewer ice-related issues. Be aware that snow slides can be sudden, so snow guards are sometimes needed near walkways.
How much does a new roof cost in New London? For most New London homes, replacing an architectural asphalt shingle roof ranges from $11,500–$24,500. The typical price lands around $19,000 for a medium-sized house, reflecting the region’s dominant single-family layouts and large roof surfaces. Higher costs often come up when there’s decking to repair, extra flashing or complex rooflines, especially in subdivisions with homes built during the 1970s–1990s now due for their second or even third roofs. Metal and flat roof replacements will cost more; if your house needs additional upgrades for attic ventilation or code-required underlayment, factor in several thousand dollars extra. How do I find a reputable roofer in New London? The crowded Chester County contractor scene means you have plenty of options, but not all are equally qualified. Reputable companies will discuss the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code and any permit needs for your township. Seek referrals from local neighbors or HOA groups—especially in the 1970s–1990s subdivisions—so you know the company has experience with your style of roof. Lastly, compare written estimates line-by-line to see who addresses all the real “hidden costs.”
Do I need a permit to replace a roof in New London? In New London, roofing permit requirements are based on the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code, as enforced by municipalities or the PA Department of Labor & Industry if your municipality has opted out. Full roof replacements involving structural deck repair will almost always require a building permit—especially in West Chester Borough and most townships. Like-for-like shingle repairs under one square are typically done without permits. Ask your roofer to confirm permit responsibilities for your specific township, and make sure their HIC registration is valid for contracts over $500; check it at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Permits protect you and ensure code compliance, so insist on documentation.
Will the crew damage my landscaping or driveway? Most reputable roofers working in New London, where driveways and yards are well-kept, use protective tarps and plywood walkways to limit damage. Dumpsters are usually placed on wood runners or boards to prevent gouging asphalt or concrete. Still, roof replacements (especially tear-offs) involve debris, nails, and foot traffic. Clarify in your written estimate how cleanup will occur and whether gutter protection will be used. Some crews include a magnetic nail sweep and will outline repair costs if anything is damaged. If your quote doesn’t mention driveway or garden protection, it’s worth asking before you sign—especially given the size and value of most properties here, where a typical roof replacement runs $11,500–$24,500.
Does attic ventilation matter in New London's climate? Yes—attic ventilation is critical in New London’s freeze-thaw climate. Without it, moist air gets trapped under the roof deck, causing plywood rot, mold, and shingle failure, particularly with Chester County’s humid summers and cold winters. Poor ventilation is a common reason for shingle warranties being voided after installation. Upgrading or correcting attic venting during roof replacement can add $800–$2,100 to your project, but skipping it is a false economy. Discuss this item with your roofer before work starts, especially in older subdivisions where original venting is frequently underperforming or was never code-compliant to begin with.
What is the difference between replacement cost and actual cash value? In Pennsylvania, and here in New London, your homeowners’ insurance policy likely uses “replacement cost” or “actual cash value” (ACV) to determine payout for roof claims. ACV means you receive the value of your roof at its depreciated age—often far less than what a new roof costs. Replacement cost covers the price to replace the roof with new materials, minus your deductible. Typically, the insurer pays ACV upfront and releases the balance after proof of completed repairs. For a typical architectural shingle replacement here, that means you may only get enough to cover half the $11,500–$24,500 if your roof is older and your policy only covers ACV. Always check your policy’s language before filing a claim.
How much does a flat roof replacement cost in New London? For flat roof sections—such as a rear porch or low-slope addition—on a New London home, you should expect to pay between $14,500–$29,500 for a professional TPO or EPDM system. This price includes removing the existing membrane, making any needed repairs to the roof deck, and updating the drainage. Lifespan for these materials is 15–25 years, depending on installation quality and ponding. Make sure your roofer includes all flashing, edge metal, and downspouts in the quote, as these are common leak points. The higher range accounts for more complex tear-offs or larger areas, which are not unusual in Chester County’s larger homes.
How can I tell if my roof decking is rotted? In New London, the era of construction—especially in 1970s–1990s developments—means plywood decking may be at the end of its life. Signs of rot include a spongy feel when you walk in the attic, visible water stains, mold, or sagging between rafters. From the outside, buckled or soft spots when walking the roof are strong indicators. Most rot only becomes obvious when shingles are torn off; a reputable roofer will build in a contingency price of $100–$140 per sheet of plywood so you’re not hit with a surprise bill. Ask for an attic inspection and be present at tear-off if you want to see decking condition firsthand.
