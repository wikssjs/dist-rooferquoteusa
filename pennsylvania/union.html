Most jobs aren’t just a few shingle patches; large expanse tear-offs with updated decks or barrier layers are common, especially as insurance claims grow after Ohio Valley storms.

Material comparison for Union homeowners

Southwestern Pennsylvania’s winters bring a ground snow load of 30 psf and single-digit January temperatures, so the way your roof sheds snow, handles freeze-thaw, and endures wind matters. Metal standing seam roofs outperform in snow country—they shed snow fast, resist ice damming, and have lifespans running 40–70 years. That said, the up-front cost ($22,000–$39,000) stops most Union homeowners from going metal except for their “forever home” or on steep pitches where snow sliding risk is highest.

The vast majority of Union homes run architectural asphalt shingles ($9,100–$19,000). Why? Post-1960 builders and suppliers in our area made these the norm, and they’re cost-effective, easy to source, and proven to handle our freeze-thaw cycles—if they’re installed right, with proper ventilation and ice barriers. Class 4 impact-resistant shingles cost more ($15,000–$19,000), but only deliver extra value if your specific lot takes repeated hail or high wind.

Flat roof membranes, like TPO or EPDM ($11,500–$23,000), are strictly for low-slope sections and commercial/garage roofs. They’re not durable enough for pitched house roofs exposed to branches unless budget and design leave no other option. For most Union homeowners, architectural asphalt offers the best balance of initial cost, cold-weather performance, and availability of experienced local crews.

Choosing a roofer in Washington County

In a busy metro market like Washington County, picking a roofer starts with checking legal basics and ends with scrutinizing their track record. Contractors must be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General for any contract over $500—always ask for their registration number and verify it at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Insurance isn't just a handshake deal, either: demand to see an up-to-date workers’ compensation certificate, which you can confirm through the WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov). This is not optional for any roof crew onsite, no matter their claims.

  • Check HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov
  • Get a copy of workers’ compensation certificate; verify at WCAIS
  • Insist on written contract per the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act
  • Confirm company address is local to Washington County
  • Ask for photos of three recent jobs in Union or adjacent townships
  • Require manufacturer credentials for the shingle or membrane being installed
  • Ask who is actually doing the roof — employees, not just ‘our crew’
  • Require explicit warranty language, both material and labor
  • Ask about rainy day/emergency tarping protocols
  • Check for EPA Lead RRP certification on pre-1978 jobs
  • Do not accept verbal estimates, especially after storms
  • Check the deposit request (should be under 15%)

Red flags here: door-knockers after hail or wind events, pressure for cash upfront, vague company info with Pittsburgh phone numbers but no local address, “waving” your deductible (illegal), and anyone unwilling to show insurance in writing. Union’s proximity to the city allows plenty of roofers in—choose the one who can prove their legitimacy.

Pennsylvania code and Washington County permit rules for roofing

Roofing work in Union falls under the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), enforced locally by the City of Washington Code Enforcement Office or the specific township’s building department, with oversight from the PA Department of Labor & Industry if a municipality opts out. In the City of Washington, a full roof replacement including a tear-off usually requires a building permit, while patch repairs on single-family homes typically do not in boroughs elsewhere in the county. Always double-check local department rules before starting work.

For contracts over $500, Pennsylvania law requires roofing contractors to be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) via the Office of Attorney General. You can— and should—verify any roofer’s HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Insurance is non-negotiable: ask for proof of workers’ compensation and confirm its validity through the WCAIS system at wcais.pa.gov before any roof crew sets foot on your property. Without these verifications, you take on unnecessary liability. A written contract is mandatory under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, and you have a three business-day right of cancellation after signing.

There is no Pennsylvania trade license specifically for roofing beyond the HIC registration; avoid anyone claiming otherwise. And for homes built before 1978, EPA Lead RRP certification applies—check at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov if you’re unsure.

What Union's housing stock means for your roof

Union’s homes track the county’s big trends: a vast portion built before 1980, with Peters and North Strabane’s suburban wave having kicked off the typical 3-bedroom, low-to-moderate pitch ranch roof. For houses put up before about 1978, you’re often dealing with plank or early plywood decking, rarely OSB. If your roof’s in the 1960s–1980s era, chances are you’ll find only a single prior shingle layer, sometimes two — not the three or more that crop up in Pittsburgh’s oldest stock. Ice and water shield wasn’t code when these places went up, so your current roof may lack eave protection against winter damming—the chief cause of leaks after a tough freeze-thaw cycle.

These homes are now at or beyond the original roof’s lifespan, meaning replacement is no longer a matter of “if,” but “when.” The good news is that, for most, you won’t get unwelcome surprises (buried slate or ancient wood shake) under the old shingles. The most common question for Union homeowners now is whether the roof deck will need spot repairs or an all-out redo; budget for a handful of sheets to be replaced if there were any past neglect or ice-dam leaks.

Why gutters get replaced with the roof

In Union, gutters aren’t just an afterthought—they’re a core part of your roof’s health. Most homes here route water down moderate slopes to the eaves. If your gutters are rotted, leaky, or pulling away, replacing just the shingles leaves half the job undone—water will back up or spill over, running down siding or dumping near the foundation. This is a real risk given the region’s clay-heavy soil and common basement moisture problems.

Modern roof installs always include new drip edge and careful flashing at eaves, which often means old gutters won’t fit back as originally installed. Many manufacturer warranties now require that water is actually channeled away—a bad gutter can void coverage for leaks you thought were roof-related. Budget $1,600–$2,900 for full gutter and downspout replacement on a standard Union ranch alongside a new roof. Remember, keeping water at least six feet from the foundation protects your house and wallet in one move.

Reading a roofing estimate line by line

A clear, honest roofing estimate in Union will spell out exactly what’s being sold—anything less should make you wary. Look first for square footage (in “squares,” where one square is 100 sq. ft.) and ensure the pitch is written out; the steeper the roof, the more work and safety equipment involved. Tear-off and disposal charges need to specify how many shingle layers will be removed and if a dumpster is included in the price. All underlayments and ice barriers must be named by product—“felt” is too generic. Decking should always be written as a cost per sheet for replacements, in case rot is uncovered; otherwise, you can get stiffed with a surprise bill. Flashing and vent boots are line items.

Good estimates separate labor (workmanship) warranty from the material warranty—most manufacturers cover defects, not installation errors. A fair payment schedule is <15% up front with the rest on completion. Don’t trust lump-sum quotes with no measurements or vague language—it’s a ploy to keep their options open and your leverage low.

Frequently asked questions — roofing in Union

Does homeowner's insurance cover roof replacement in Union? Homeowner’s insurance in Union may cover roof replacement if damage is from a covered peril such as hail or wind—a common claim source in Washington County’s storm-prone corridor. Insurers typically don’t pay for age-related wear or neglect. If a recent hail event shredded your shingles, document everything before calling in a claim. Insurers will send out an adjuster, and most will pay “actual cash value” unless your policy has replacement cost coverage. One thing to note: Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act prohibits contractors from “waiving” your deductible—don’t trust anyone offering to “eat it.” For a typical architectural shingle replacement, your total job cost is likely to fall within $9,100–$19,000, but you’re on the hook for your deductible and any upgrades.
Do I need to leave my house during a roof replacement? In almost every Union roof replacement, you can remain in your home. Crews work outside, and while the banging and roof debris removal can be loud, there’s little risk to personal safety inside unless your attic has exposed hazards. If you work from home or have pets sensitive to noise, you may want to plan those two to three days elsewhere. For families with small children or anyone sensitive to dust or allergens, communicate with your contractor about protection protocols—especially on pre-1978 homes where the EPA Lead RRP Rule applies. For an average asphalt re-roof ($9,100–$19,000), expect disruption for two days for most Union ranches, sometimes a bit longer if decking replacement is required.
What causes ice dams on Union roofs? Ice dams in Union are the result of freeze-thaw cycles and poorly insulated attics—common with homes built before building code required ice and water shield at the eaves. As snow melts from roof heat loss and refreezes at the cold eave, water pools and backs up under shingles. This quickly leads to leaks, interior ceiling stains, and even deck rot, especially if your roof lacks modern barrier layers. Most local replacements correct this by running ice/water shield 3+ feet up from the eaves and checking attic insulation. Repairing an active ice dam leak can cost $475–$1,800, but the real fix comes at roof replacement. If you’re in Union and see thick icicles, it’s a sign of trouble developing on your roof.
What's the cheapest way to replace a roof in Union? If you’re looking to minimize cost for a full roof replacement in Union, stick with straightforward architectural asphalt shingles—the material that dominates our region’s housing and is best supported by local suppliers and crews. On a basic single-story ranch, and if the old decking is mostly solid, you’ll typically be in the $9,100–$19,000 range. Avoid unnecessary upgrades: skip premium underlayments and stick to a manufacturer-value line. Don’t layer new shingles over old—double-layer jobs are discouraged and can backfire at resale or in storms. If you’re offered anything cheaper, get it in writing but check against HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov; too-good-to-be-true pricing usually omits labor, insurance, or proper disposal.
How can I tell if my roof decking is rotted? In Union, the age of many homes means your roof may have original plank or early plywood decking. Signs of rot include spongy roof spots underfoot (only check from inside on a safe attic platform), visible sagging between rafters, dark or water-stained sheathing in the attic, or musty smells. During re-roofing, reputable contractors price decking replacement per sheet—typically discovered only after the old shingles come off. If you hire a roofer willing to skip decking checks to lower estimates, expect short-term savings and long-term headaches. When the time comes to replace rotted wood alongside shingles, expect it priced as a per-sheet add-on in your estimate, which might run a few hundred dollars per sheet on top of the shingle cost ($9,100–$19,000 range for the full job).
How much does a new roof cost in Union? For the vast majority of Union homeowners with single-family homes, a full architectural asphalt shingle replacement falls in the $9,100–$19,000 range, with most landing around $15,000 in recent years. Steeper, cut-up roofs or larger square footage will push toward the high end. Metal standing seam has become more common on forever homes, but the starting cost is substantially higher ($22,000–$39,000). Flat roof replacements on garages or additions cost $11,500–$23,000. Repairs, like leak fixing or flashing work, start at $475 and go up based on scope. Always demand a line-item quote so you know what’s included.
Are there historic district rules affecting roofing in Union? Union itself isn’t known for strict historic district oversight the way parts of the City of Washington might be, but if you’re near any local landmarks or in an area with an HOA or township architectural committee, always check before roof work. None of the county-level or state-level rules restrict your roof color or material as long as you’re following the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC) and local permitting. Most jobs—especially on the common 1960s–80s ranches and splits—won’t run into any architectural restrictions. Full replacements run $9,100–$19,000 for asphalt, so factor in any cosmetic upgrades if you’re in one of the rare neighborhoods with material or color controls.
Are storm-chasing roofers a problem in Union? Yes, after every major hail or windstorm that sweeps through the Ohio Valley, Union sees a spike in out-of-town “storm chaser” roofing outfits. These are often aggressive canvassers who pressure you for “free” inspections or urge you to sign an insurance claim on the spot. Since the market here is close to Pittsburgh, scammers sometimes slip in, doing sub-par work or disappearing before warranty callbacks. Always check HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov before signing anything and never pay a deposit over 15%. For standard storm damage patching or replacement, repairs typically run $475–$1,800 and full replacements start at $9,100. Deal with a company who has a traceable local address—door-knockers often do not.
What is the difference between TPO and EPDM? Both TPO (thermoplastic polyolefin) and EPDM (ethylene propylene diene monomer) are flat roof membranes used in Union for low-slope sections—garages, porch roofs, and some commercial spaces. TPO is a white, heat-welded product that reflects summer heat, while EPDM is a black, rubber-like sheet glued or ballasted in place. TPO can better handle ponding water for brief periods, while EPDM excels in flexibility and cold cracks. Both do well with our region’s snow load and freeze-thaw cycles so long as seams are kept intact. Each shares a similar price range here—$11,500–$23,000 installed for a replacement on a residential roof. The right choice comes down to your installer’s experience rather than material marketing, so hire based on references and local flat roof experience.
