Clearfield County roofing prices by material and scope — 2026

Clearfield County’s roofing market is shaped by the sheer number of older single-family homes—most built between 1940 and 1970, according to recent data, with nearly three-quarters of housing stock constructed before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). This means many roofs here are at, or beyond, their design lifespan, with a heavy presence of architectural asphalt shingles installed over plank decking. The need for full tear-off and replacement, retrofitting over plank, or upgrading to modern systems is common as Clearfield County property owners try to protect their homes and keep insurance coverage. Of 32,673 residents in the county’s core municipalities, about 9,200 households own their homes—so roof replacement is not a luxury but a routine, recurring project.

In Clearfield County, typical asphalt shingle replacements rely on cost-effective architectural products, but you’ll also see some homes—especially recent upgrades—switching to impact-resistant shingles or even standing seam metal for better wind defense along exposed ridge lines. Flat roofs are in the mix too, due to porches and additions. Roofers operating here factor in travel from the more populated DuBois–Jefferson area, sometimes adding modest surcharges. The price you pay covers regionally-sourced materials, local code requirements, and labor that understands these houses and weather loads.

TPO and EPDM on Clearfield County homes

In Clearfield County, many homes feature a flat roof segment—a porch, low-slope addition, or rear bump-out—alongside a standard pitched roof. These flat sections require a single-ply membrane, such as TPO or EPDM, instead of shingles because shingles can’t reliably shed water below a 3/12 pitch. When shingles are installed on a 2/12 slope, water finds its way beneath, leading to leaks, rot, and costly structural repair. Ponding water is a particular risk in our region, trapping moisture and shortening roof life. Membrane systems are formulated for standing water, providing watertight seams and flexible coverage for complicated roof shapes.

It’s not uncommon for Clearfield contractors to encounter porches or small additions with improperly shingled low slopes. If a roofer recommends shingles for your 2/12 addition, you’re getting clear evidence of inexperience or a willingness to cut corners. Membrane roofs like TPO and EPDM typically last 15–25 years in Clearfield County’s freeze-thaw cycles and cost $11,000–$22,000 depending on square footage and detail work (curbs, drains, transitions). Expect to pay toward the higher end for anything with complicated penetrations or where the substrate is in poor shape. If you want to avoid recurring leaks, choose membrane over shingles for low-slope roofs here.

Roofing permits in Clearfield County: what you need before work starts

Roofing projects in Clearfield County are regulated under the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), which follows the International Residential Code as its base standard. Permitting requirements depend on the municipality. In the City of DuBois, any structural roof replacement or changes to roof framing members trigger a building permit, issued by the City of DuBois Code Enforcement Office. Routine shingle replacement—where the sheathing and structure remain intact—usually does not require a permit in most townships, including Sandy Township. However, if you live in a township, permits and inspection routines are handled by your own municipal code officer, so it’s wise to confirm before signing any contract.

Pennsylvania requires Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for roofing projects over $500. Always verify your contractor’s registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov, where you can crosscheck their credentials. Written contracts are not optional—they are mandated by the state for jobs over $500, and you retain a 3-business-day right to cancel under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA).

Workers’ compensation is mandatory for any employer on your roof. Use WCAIS at wcais.pa.gov to confirm a contractor’s insurance status. Between PA’s UCC, HIC registration, and insurance verification, the system revolves around your protection as a homeowner. Don’t move forward with someone unwilling to provide these details—they are not following Clearfield County standards.

Gutters, drainage and roof longevity in Clearfield County

Your gutter and downspout system is as important as the roof itself in Clearfield County’s long heating season and snow-prone climate. Water from melting snow and heavy rains needs proper channels—starting at the drip edge, flowing through gutters, and out downspouts set well away from the home’s foundation. Local housing often sits over fieldstone or block basements, so if gutters discharge too close to the house, you’ll face moisture intrusion and even basement flooding. Many roof warranties are voided if poor drainage is causing premature shingle failure or ice damming at the eaves.

Replacing a roof without upgrading aging or damaged gutters is only half a fix. Shingle runoff can overwhelm undersized, clogged, or mispitched gutters, leading to fascia rot and landscape erosion. In Clearfield County, it’s common practice to price gutter replacement as part of a full roofing quote. For a standard home, expect typical gutter replacement costs to add $1,200–$2,400 alongside a new shingle roof, depending on linear footage and complexity. Gutter protection systems, like guards, help keep leaves and ice out—especially valuable with the county’s mix of mature maples and oaks dropping debris. Quality roofers look at the water system as a whole, not just the surface you see.

How to compare roofing quotes in Clearfield County

A Clearfield County roofing quote should leave nothing to guesswork. Every estimate needs to state the roof area in “squares” (one square = 100 sq. ft.), with the main slope’s pitch clearly listed. The tear-off and disposal of old shingles—often two or more layers on 1940s–1970s plank decks—must be itemized, including dumpster fees. Underlayment and ice barrier products should be listed by actual brand or spec, not generic “felt” or “paper.” Since a majority of homes here have plank, not plywood, decking, the quote must include a per-sheet contingency if rotten wood is found. Every detail—flashings, vent boots, pipe collars—should have coverage noted.

Warranties should be twofold: the shingle or membrane manufacturer’s, and a separate line for workmanship (labor) years. Payment schedules should keep upfront deposits modest (no more than 15%), with the balance due only after completion and inspection. If your quote lumps everything as one big number, you’re not getting true information—without materials, areas, and scope called out, there’s no way to compare apples to apples. It may hide shortcuts or leave you holding the bag when change orders pile up.

Repair or replace? A decision guide for Clearfield County roofs

Homeowners in Clearfield County often face the repair-or-replace question, especially on older homes. If your asphalt shingle roof is under 15 years old and a single leak appears—often around flashing or a vent—repair is absolutely reasonable. But most county homes were built well before 1980, and original roofs or 20-year overlays are out of runway. When leaks show up in multiple spots or decking is soft underfoot, replacement is the value move. Chasing repeated repairs—at $1,100 each—quickly overtakes the cost of a new roof. For example, three repairs inside five years ($1,100 × 3 = $3,300) still leave you with rotten wood and ugly ceilings, but do nothing for resale or warranty.

Storms and wind along the northern ridge lines trigger urgent decisions. If you see blown-off shingles or ridge cap failures, a documented insurance claim often pays most of the bill—don’t accept patchwork if more than a plane or slope is damaged. Flat roof issues, like ponding or blisters, should be met with full membrane replacement. One more Pennsylvania reality: if you’re leaking as winter comes on, emergency tarping or a tight patch ($450–$1,750) is worth every dime until a full job is possible. What insurance covers on a Clearfield County roof

Homeowners insurance can help with Clearfield County roof replacements and repairs—but only in cases of sudden, accidental damage like wind, hail, or fallen trees. If your roof suffers storm damage, document everything before you clean up: take photos from different angles, grab a shot of any water stains, and save any fragments of shingle or debris. Next, notify your insurer and request an adjuster. The claims process in Pennsylvania means the insurer sends out an adjuster, who will draft a written “scope of loss.” You’ll see two numbers: actual cash value (what your old roof is worth today) and replacement cost (what it should take to fully restore it).

You’ll need to pay your policy deductible directly. Some contractors will offer to “waive” or rebate the deductible, but this is specifically prohibited in Pennsylvania and falls under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA). If a roofer suggests this, walk away: it risks your claim and far more. Always insist on a written contract (required above $500) and do not sign an agreement that hides the deductible amount.

If you use insurance funds for your roof, stick closely to the scope issued by your adjuster. Upgrades—such as switching from architectural shingles ($8,700–$18,000) to standing seam metal ($21,000–$37,500)—may not be covered in full. Finally, remember that damage due to long-term wear and tear or poor maintenance isn’t covered. Only sudden events create a legitimate claim in Clearfield County.

Snow load, ice dams and wind in Clearfield County

Clearfield County sits atop some of Pennsylvania’s highest snow loads—40 pounds per square foot—and routinely faces a winter design temperature of 2 °F. In practice, this means roofs must bear deep snow, withstand repeated freeze-thaw cycles, and stand up to strong ridge-line winds, especially along the Allegheny Plateau in the county’s north. Ice dams are a regular threat: heat from inside the home melts snow high on the roof, refreezing once it reaches the cold, uninsulated eave. Dammed water then backs up beneath shingles, causing leaks that bypass even healthy roofing. Without an ice/water shield at the eaves, repair bills can quickly reach $1,100 for interior damage—often much more.

Along northern ridges, wind-driven shingle uplift is another local hazard. Ridge cap failures and missing shingles are common—especially on older architectural products reaching end of life. Hail, though less frequent, can bruise shingles by crushing the asphalt granules and softening the mat below, which does not always leave visible holes but can still drive leaks and the need for a full replacement. A typical full replacement here runs $8,700–$18,000. Local roofers favor self-adhering underlayments and wide eave protection due to Clearfield County’s long heating season and exposure. A roof strategy here is not about the storms on paper—it’s about the snow and wind the house takes in real terms, season after season.

Does roof pitch affect the price in Clearfield County? Yes, pitch has a significant impact on roofing costs in Clearfield County. The steeper the roof, the more labor and safety measures are required, which increases the price. Homes in DuBois and surrounding townships often feature traditional medium slopes, but any steep pitch (above 7/12) generally adds crew hours and requires planking or scaffolding. Low-pitch roofs don’t allow for shingles at all; porches or additions with pitches under 3/12 must use membrane systems such as TPO or EPDM, which range from $11,000–$22,000 depending on size and complexity. If your quote for a steep roof doesn’t reflect extra labor or equipment charges—or if you see shingle pricing on a low slope—it’s worth questioning the roofer’s understanding of local standards. Always get the pitch listed in writing to ensure an accurate estimate for your Clearfield project.
How do I file a storm damage roof claim in Pennsylvania? In Pennsylvania, start by documenting all damage before any temporary repairs are made—take clear photos of the entire roof, close-ups of the damage, and pictures of anything affected inside the house. Notify your insurance agent right away and request an insurance adjuster’s inspection. The adjuster will create a scope of loss, which your chosen contractor should match in their proposal. You’ll receive funds based on either the actual cash value or replacement cost of your roof, less your deductible (for example, a typical architectural shingle replacement is $8,700–$18,000). Remember: it’s illegal under Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act for a contractor to “waive” your deductible—any offer to do so is a red flag that could jeopardize your claim. Insist on a written contract and make sure deductible handling is above board to stay fully protected under state law.
Are there historic district rules affecting roofing in Clearfield County? Clearfield County does not have county-wide historic district overlay regulations for roofing. If you’re in doubt about neighborhood rules, you can always call your local municipal code officer for clarity, but for most Clearfield County properties, replacement roofing is governed only by standard building code and state contract rules.

Is a metal roof worth the extra cost in Clearfield County? A standing seam metal roof is a significant investment in Clearfield County—typically $21,000–$37,500 installed—but it easily outlasts asphalt, particularly on wind-exposed ridge lines. Metal resists the uplift and ridge cap failures common on northern slopes and sheds snow efficiently in our 40 psf snow belt. If you plan to keep the house more than 20 years, or if hail/wind are routine headaches, metal provides both peace of mind and long-term savings. For typical insurance replacements or flips, architectural shingles may be more budget-friendly, but permanent homes, farmhouses, or higher-value properties along exposed areas make good candidates for metal. Just make sure proper snow guards and ventilation are installed to match local winter requirements.

However, climbing onto a 1940–1970s wood-frame roof can be risky—old decking, hidden rot, or steep slopes may not be safe, especially with the snow load and ridge winds we see locally. What underlayment should be used on a Clearfield County roof? Most Clearfield County roofs should get synthetic underlayment or a high-quality felt for the main field, plus a self-adhering ice and water barrier at all eaves and valleys. If your home has valleys, dormers, or penetrations (pipes, chimneys), those spots get special attention. Homeowner’s insurance in Clearfield County pays for roof replacement only if the damage comes from a covered “sudden” event—such as wind, hail, or a falling tree—not just aging and wear. Is ice barrier underlayment required in Clearfield County? Under the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC)—which is enforced locally in all Clearfield County municipalities—it’s standard practice to install self-adhering ice and water shield at all eaves, valleys, and critical transition points. What causes ice dams on Clearfield County roofs? Ice dams occur on Clearfield County roofs when indoor heat escapes into the attic, melting snow from underneath. That water then runs down to the eaves—where there’s no house heat—and refreezes along the gutter edge. Once a ridge forms, melting snow backs up under the shingles and leaks through the decking, causing stains, rot, and costly repairs (repairs can run $450–$1,750). Our local climate, with a long heating season, frequent subfreezing nights, and heavy snow load, means any insulation gaps or ventilation failures almost guarantee repeat ice dams. Underlayment that runs up the roof past the interior wall line is vital—never accept a quote omitting ice barrier, especially if your house has trouble spots under valleys or dormers. Preventing ice dams in Clearfield County is about attic airflow, eave barrier, and whole-system drainage.
