Roofing Replacement & Repair costs in Pike County — 2026

Pike County’s 41,527 residents make up a mostly owner-occupied, single-family market, with older homes concentrated in some tracts — on average, a third of the homes were built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). This impacts the kind of roofing work done here: Many subdivisions around Dingman’s Ferry were built in the 1980s–1990s, and now those 25–40-year-old asphalt roofs have reached the end of their working life. As a result, large replacement projects in these tracts are common, not just spot repairs.

The local roofing market is thin and covers a broad swath of rural properties, so prices here reflect commuting labor from Scranton–Wilkes-Barre and delivery costs from Scranton-area suppliers. Most Pike County homes use architectural asphalt shingles because they balance cost and availability. Isolated repair jobs, often caused by Delaware River ridge-line wind that lifts shingle tabs around Milford, stay on the lower end of the price scale, while full replacements in older subdivisions rarely come in at the minimum due to roof square footage and secondary repairs (decking, ventilation).

What Pike County's climate does to a roof

In Pike County, the Delaware River ridge puts homes in the path of wind uplift and sudden weather shifts that aren’t seen in counties further west. Shingle tab tearing from wind is a direct result: wind funnels up slopes and over ridgelines near Milford, causing the exposed edges of architectural shingles to curl or break loose. Even new roofs can shed tabs under these conditions, exposing the underlayment beneath — a problem that often lands repairs in the $500–$1,900 range annually if left unchecked.

Winter is another story. With a ground snow load of 40 psf and a winter design temperature of 4 °F, Pike County roofs bear more weight and deeper cold than most of Pennsylvania. The risk here is not just collapse (rare on one- or two-story homes), but ice damming at the eaves. When heat leaks through poorly-insulated attics, snow against a cold eave edge refreezes into a dam. Water pools behind the dam and works under shingles, soaking the roof deck and wicking into insulation or walls. Repairs from this failure mode can quickly exceed a simple patch — a leak from ice damming may run into a $1,900 repair or be the first warning that drives a full roof replacement in the $9,400–$20,000 range.

In Pike, hail is less common than wind or ice, but when it hits, the damage can be subtle: bruised shingle matting that weakens the roof months before leaks appear. This isn’t visible from the ground, and can void a manufacturer warranty if left exposed. Class 4 impact-resistant shingles hold up better, but the upfront cost climbs to $15,500–$20,000.

Roofing warranties in Pike County: what's actually covered

Most Pike County roofing projects come with two separate warranties, though many homeowners assume they’re the same. The first is the manufacturer’s warranty, which covers the shingles or membrane against materials defects. These warranties are typically prorated — meaning the payout for a failed shingle decreases every year you own it — and they rarely cover costs to tear off and reinstall. Transferability can be limited: many shingle manufacturers require the new owner to register the transfer within a set window after resale, or the warranty drops down to basic coverage, if it’s honored at all. Not registering in time voids the enhanced warranty entirely.

The second warranty is the contractor’s workmanship guarantee. In Pike County, as elsewhere in Pennsylvania, this warranty is only as strong as the company that provides it. If a contractor offers a 25-year workmanship warranty but has only been in business for two years, the real protection you get may be less reliable than a shorter but well-backed guarantee from an established company. Most workmanship warranties in the area range from five to fifteen years, and require that the roof be installed to code, with adequate ventilation and no layering over old shingles. Roofing over old materials or blocking attic vents usually voids both manufacturer and installer coverage, even if you have paperwork in hand.

Deadlines matter. Some warranties require registration within 30–60 days of installation. Always read the fine print on both types, and ask who to call for claims if things go wrong — especially with contractors who commute in from outside Pike County.

Repair or replace? A decision guide for Pike County roofs

Homeowners in Pike County should be direct: for architectural roofs installed in the 1980s–1990s, you're simply at the end of the product life cycle. Trying to nurse a 30-year-old roof through another season with patches costs more in the long run, especially considering how shingles in this area fail by tab loss and ice-dam leaks. If your roof is under 15 years old and there’s a single leak (from a pipe boot, for example), a repair in the $500–$1,900 band makes sense — especially in sections along US Route 6 where heavy winds can snap tabs on just one slope. But pay for this repair more than twice in five years and you’ll cross the cost of a new roof, especially as soft decking and wet insulation are revealed at each tear-off.

For roofs past the 20–25-year mark, especially in large tracts around Dingman’s Ferry, ongoing repairs add up fast. A few $1,200 repairs and interior fixes push you right into the range of a full replacement at $9,400–$20,000. If your roof suffered wind or hail in a storm corridor — or if you’re heading into winter with an active leak — don’t defer. Wet wood won’t cure over winter. See the recommendations below:

Why gutters get replaced with the roof

Replacing a roof in Pike County without addressing gutters and drainage almost always means leaving out half the job. The roof, gutters, downspouts, drip edge, and even grading around the foundation work as a single water-control system. When shingles are replaced, new metal drip edge is set at the eaves and rakes — this is where old gutters (bent, undersized, or nailed into rotted fascia) often don’t fit correctly and start to leak shortly after installation. Old gutters that dump water too close to the foundation put you at risk for basement seepage, especially in homes built along hilly tracts in Milford or neighborhoods near streambeds in Dingman Township.

Not only do many manufacturer warranties require proper drainage to stay valid, but a new roof can actually fail early if meltwater or rain is not quickly moved away. In Pike County, full gutter replacement with the roof typically adds $2,100–$3,800 for standard K-style aluminum on an average single-story house, including downspouts and proper discharge extensions. Skipping this step often means calling a roofer — or a basement waterproofer — within three years to patch what could have been done right at the start.

Decking, ventilation and flashing — the hidden roof costs

Many Pike County homeowners are surprised at the “extras” that push a roofing project above the low end of any quote. Rotted decking isn’t visible until shingles are off and the crew inspects the plywood or planking underneath. In a county with 34.8% of homes built before 1980, hidden rot is common: expect $85–$115 per sheet for OSB or plywood replacement, which for a typical roof can quickly stack up into the $1,000–$2,000 range.

Attic ventilation is the second hidden cost — and it’s vital here because poor airflow leads directly to ice dams in this climate. If your attic isn’t vented to code, shingle warranties become void and new ice dams can defeat even a brand-new roof. Upgrading to ridge vent or new soffit intake can add $1,050–$1,900, depending on scope.

Flashing and vent boots are often overlooked in cheap quotes. Chimney and wall flashings, especially on homes along hillsides or in roof valleys around Delaware Township, are trickier to install. New flashing typically adds $450–$950 per chimney or wall intersection. If a quote ignores these, expect leaks and callbacks. Finally, drip edge and roof-to-wall transition work is built into quality estimates but rarely detailed in discount bids.

Reading a roofing estimate line by line

Reading an estimate in Pike County means demanding specifics. Every quote should break out the area (in “squares,” where one square = 100 sq ft), pitch (steep roofs are more labor-intensive), tear-off and disposal, underlayment, ice and water shield, decking contingencies, flashing and vent boots, and the labor warranty (distinct from the shingle warranty). Underlayment should be stated by name and coverage — “synthetic underlayment” or “ice shield 6’ at eaves,” not generic “felt.” If decking replacement costs are missing, you may end up with surprise add-ons after the fact.

Beware of vague “lump sum” quotes without square count or product type. If you can’t compare apples to apples, you’re comparing a detailed job to a cut corner. The payment schedule matters: in Pike County, reasonable contractors ask for a deposit of no more than 15% up front, with balance due on completion or after inspection — never large upfront cash. Pennsylvania code and Pike County permit rules for roofing

For residential roofing work in Pike County, the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), based on the IRC, sets the minimum standards. These are enforced at the municipal level — for example, Milford Borough has its own Code Enforcement Office, while other townships have their own permitting offices. For homes in Milford Borough, a building permit is required if you replace more than 25% of the structural roof deck. If you’re just replacing shingles (like-for-like) and the repair is under one roofing square, Dingman Township typically does not require a permit, but you should confirm with the township office before work starts.

Roofing contractors in Pennsylvania must be registered under the Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) program for contracts over $500. You can — and should — verify registration status yourself at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Contractor insurance is another non-negotiable. Pennsylvania law requires all employers to carry workers’ compensation, which can be checked through the state’s WCAIS database at wcais.pa.gov. These checks are the only practical protection against unlicensed or uninsured work, since there is no trade-specific roofing license in Pennsylvania.

Finally, if your home was built before 1978 and will have painted surfaces disturbed by a roofing contractor, federal EPA Lead RRP rules apply — verify their certification through certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov.

Frequently asked questions — roofing in Pike County

How do I verify a roofing contractor is registered in Pennsylvania?

In Pennsylvania, any contractor bidding on a job in Pike County valued over $500 must be registered under the Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) law. You can verify this registration yourself at hic.attorneygeneral.gov by searching for the contractor’s business name or HIC number. If you can’t find them in the database, they aren’t properly registered — and you shouldn’t hire them. This protects you under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, which requires a written contract and gives you a 3-day cancellation right. Registration is not the same as trade licensing: there’s no roofing license in Pennsylvania, but HIC registration is required for all legal roofing contracts, including full replacements in the $9,400–$20,000 range. Always ask for proof of workers’ compensation insurance, which you can verify through WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). Never pay a deposit until these checks are complete.

How do I know if my Pike County roof needs replacing?

Pike County homes—especially those in older subdivisions near Dingman’s Ferry and Milford—often hit the replacement threshold at 25–30 years. Look for shingle tabs missing from wind along the Delaware River ridge, chronic leaks after snowmelt (suggesting ice damming), or patches where granules are worn away. One-off repairs in the $500–$1,900 range can buy you a few seasons for a younger roof, but if you see active leaks on a roof installed before 2000, or repairs are eating into your budget every year, it’s likely time for a full replacement in the $9,400–$20,000 band. Get an inspection if you're unsure—a reputable roofer can tell you honestly.

Does roof pitch affect the price in Pike County?

Yes, pitch is a major factor in Pike County roofing costs. Steeper roofs (greater than 6/12) are much harder to work on and require more labor, more staging, and extra safety gear—especially in the hilly neighborhoods around Milford. Roofers will factor this into your quote, and it pushes the total job up the pricing band. For example, a two-story steep-pitch roof needing architectural shingle replacement may hit the high end of the $9,400–$20,000 range, while a low-pitch ranch roof of the same size lands much closer to the bottom. Always ask contractors to state your roof’s pitch in “squares” to compare apples to apples.

What happens if rotted decking is found after tear-off?

When reroofing in Pike County—especially on homes built before 1980, which make up about 34.8% of local inventory—it’s common to find rotted decking under old shingles. Typically, contractors will price decking replacement per sheet (4x8 sheet of OSB or plywood), usually at $85–$115 per sheet. For a roof showing signs of leaks or past ice damming, this can add $1,000–$2,000 to the job if several sheets are needed. Good estimates list this as a contingency up front. Weak quotes usually don’t, leading to sticker shock after tear-off. Always clarify before work starts, and ask the crew to document replacement with photos.

How much does a new roof cost in Pike County?

A full architectural asphalt shingle replacement on a typical Pike County home—most commonly single-family and about 1,800–2,400 square feet—generally falls between $9,400–$20,000. Metal standing seam roofs start at $23,000 and can go over $40,500. These numbers reflect rural labor, delivery from Scranton suppliers, and the real cost of code-compliant installation including tear-off, underlayment, and disposal. If you’re seeing prices much lower, it’s usually because of shortcuts on ventilation, underlayment, or insurance. Always compare detailed, line-item estimates for an apples-to-apples view.

Can I install new shingles over old ones in Pennsylvania?

You can, but it’s not recommended in Pike County for several reasons. Pennsylvania’s UCC allows for a single layer of overlay, but many local codes—and manufacturer warranties—require complete tear-off to inspect decking and install proper underlayment. Roof-overs hide rot, ice damming damage, and produce poor shingle adhesion under the weight of a new layer. Most insurance policies and all leading shingle warranties are void on overlays, costing you protection on a roof with a replacement value of $9,400–$20,000 or more. If a contractor pushes overlays as “code compliant,” ask what’s being hidden beneath—and get other quotes.

How do I find a reputable roofer in Pike County?

Start by verifying the Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov and ask for proof of workers’ compensation insurance from WCAIS. Local reputation matters: Pike County is rural, and most contractors commute in from Scranton–Wilkes-Barre. Ask for addresses of three local jobs and check them yourself. Compare detailed, written estimates—line by line—never a lump sum. Avoid firms asking for more than a 15% deposit. A reputable installer will break out labor, material, decking, and disposal. For a full replacement of $9,400–$20,000, don't cut corners with a nonlocal or unregistered crew. Ask neighbors and check for reviews mentioning projects in Milford or Dingman areas—ignore fly-by-night “storm” firms canvassing after wind events.

Will filing a roof claim raise my premium?

Filing a roof claim after storm or hail damage in Pike County does not instantly mean your premium will increase, but it can. Insurers consider the area’s local weather pattern and frequency of claims in your zip code, not just your individual record. If entire subdivisions are re-roofed after a major wind event, as sometimes happens along the Delaware River valley, premiums countywide may adjust in coming years. A single claim for a full replacement roof (say, $15,500–$20,000 for architectural shingles) is less likely to spike your rate than multiple claims or an obvious pattern of deferred maintenance. Always check with your agent before filing, and keep records of all repairs and invoices.

How long do architectural shingles last compared to 3-tab?

Architectural shingles installed in Pike County are quoted at a 25–30 year lifespan, while old-style 3-tab shingles rarely make it past 16–18 years given our snow loads and ridge-line winds. Most homes getting replaced now in the Dingman’s Ferry area had 3-tabs installed in the 1980s–1990s, and these typically failed early due to wind and ice damage. Modern architectural shingles are heavier and designed for Northeast climates — their warranties are stronger but do require proper installation and attic ventilation. The small cost premium (roof replacement in the $9,400–$20,000 range) will pay for itself in longevity and lower maintenance, especially with Pike’s climate.

What is the difference between replacement cost and actual cash value?

Replacement cost coverage pays for a new roof of comparable material and workmanship, up to the full installed price — typically $9,400–$20,000 for Pike County shingle roofs — minus your deductible. Actual cash value (ACV) pays out what your roof is worth today, factoring in age, wear, and depreciation. A 25-year-old shingle roof with ACV coverage might net you just a few thousand toward a new installation, while replacement cost covers the whole project. Always check your policy details before filing a claim after a wind or hail event in Pike County — and remember, insurers may send an adjuster who is not familiar with local wind or ice damming issues. Clarifying your coverage can prevent surprises after storm damage.

Does Pike County get enough hail to justify Class 4 shingles?

While Pike County roofs are vulnerable to wind and ice damage along the Delaware River valley, hail of the size and frequency seen in western or central Pennsylvania is less common here. Class 4 impact-resistant shingles cost more — expect $15,500–$20,000 for a typical architectural install — and may earn you a small insurance discount. However, for most homes in Milford, Dingman, or Blooming Grove, the bigger risk is shingle loss from wind or ice damage, not hail. Unless you’ve had repeated hail claims or your insurer credits the upgrade, standard architectural shingles usually offer stronger value for the extra cost in this market.
