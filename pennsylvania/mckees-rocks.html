Roof age and construction era in McKees Rocks

Roofing projects in McKees Rocks almost always reveal the history of Allegheny County’s housing wave from 1900–1950. Over 88% of homes here were built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), which means you are more likely to find plank decking under the shingles instead of modern plywood or OSB. These older houses usually have moderate pitches—common for Western Pennsylvania—so roofs shed snow well, but the decking can be gapped or worn after decades of freeze-thaw. It’s typical to find at least two layers of shingles on top during a tear-off, which isn’t allowed under today’s codes but was a normal practice up until the late 20th century.

On the oldest homes, you probably have minimal or no ice barrier installed at the eaves, because it was not required at the time. Original roofs were sometimes installed right over spaced plank boards, so many jobs involve replacing a surprising amount of rotten or split wood, especially along edges and valleys. If your house is one of the rare post-1980 builds, you might get by with only a single layer of shingles to remove, and you’ll likely have all-plywood decking and better underlayments. Still, most quotes here will assume extra attention to hidden decay and code upgrades, because the local housing stock simply demands it. Anyone quoting you for just a nail-over likely hasn’t been up in enough McKees Rocks attics.

Pennsylvania code and Allegheny County permit rules for roofing

Roofing work in McKees Rocks falls under the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), which is based on the International Residential Code. Code enforcement for Pittsburgh and the rest of Allegheny County sits with the Department of Permits, Licenses, and Inspections. For homes in this area, replaced roofing that covers more than 50% of the structure’s roof area typically requires a building permit. On the other hand, like-for-like shingle repairs that cover a small area can be completed without a permit.

Pennsylvania requires all contractors performing home improvement jobs over $500 to be registered with the Office of Attorney General as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC). You can and should verify any roofer’s HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov before hiring. Employers in Pennsylvania must carry workers’ compensation insurance, which you can check online at WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). Contractors cannot waive or absorb your insurance deductible, and all roofing contracts over $500 must be in writing per the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act.

If your home was built before 1978, make sure the contractor has an EPA Lead RRP certification for any work disturbing painted surfaces—find certified renovators at certifiedrenovatorsearch.epa.gov.

Reading the warranty on a McKees Rocks roof

Every new roof in McKees Rocks comes with two separate warranties, and it’s crucial to know which is which. First, the manufacturer warranty covers material defects—think shingle blistering or early granule loss—and is almost always prorated over time. This means if your 30-year shingles fail in year fifteen, you’ll get only partial coverage for materials, and nothing for the cost of labor. Most brands today offer limited lifetime warranties, but require registration—sometimes within 30 or 60 days of installation—or you get only a baseline level of coverage.

The second is the contractor’s workmanship warranty, covering leaks or issues caused by improper installation. This term is as strong as the company’s track record. A 25-year labor warranty from a brand-new crew is far less meaningful than 10 years from a roofer who’s been around for decades in Allegheny County. Workmanship coverage is often voided by things like insufficient attic ventilation or if new shingles are applied over old ones. If you sell the house, check if the warranty transfers—many require a fee or written notice within a set time to remain valid for the next owner.

Snow load, ice dams and wind in McKees Rocks

Allegheny County’s 30 psf ground snow load and regular Ohio Valley storms have direct impact on how your roof should be built and maintained in McKees Rocks. Ice dams form when rising heat melts the lower layer of roof snow, which then refreezes at the eaves. This process is driven by periods where the outside temperature hovers above and below freezing—common with our winter design temperature of 8 °F and frequent freeze-thaw cycles on hilly Pittsburgh terrain. The result: water backs up under shingles, soaks plank boards, and leaks into attics or ceilings. Skipping a proper ice barrier at the eaves is gambling with leaks that can require a $475–$1,800 repair—or, in severe cases, a complete replacement at $8,900–$19,000.

Wind events from Ohio Valley storm tracks drive tree limbs against roofs and lift shingles along ridges and eaves. Even if hail doesn’t create obvious holes, it can bruise asphalt matting beneath the surface, reducing shingle lifespan without signs until next season’s freeze causes granule loss. For this reason, impact-resistant shingles—though pricier—see real demand here, especially where large, old trees hang over houses built pre-1950. The extra cost for these shingles often falls between $14,500–$19,000 for a full replacement but is justified by reduced risk of repeat repairs. If you’re quoted a bargain price without the right underlayments, you’re not getting a roof built for McKees Rocks conditions.

Flat roof sections: the part most quotes get wrong

Many McKees Rocks homes have a flat or low-slope section—whether it’s a rear addition, porch, or a bump-out over a kitchen. These areas cannot use standard asphalt shingles. Shingles on slopes under 3/12 (and especially 2/12 or less) will fail rapidly here, letting ponding water and freeze-thaw cycles sneak water underneath and into your wood decking.

Proper flat roof membranes like TPO or EPDM are specifically made for these conditions, shedding water off even shallow pitches and tolerating puddles that would destroy shingle tabs. Lifespans are shorter than on pitched architectural shingle roofs—usually 15–25 years—but a correctly installed membrane system in McKees Rocks still outlasts any shingled attempt on a low-slope porch. Flat roof membrane replacements typically land between $11,500–$22,500 for a whole section, reflecting both the material and labor involved. If you get a shingle quote for a 2/12 slope, that’s a strong sign to keep looking for a roofer who knows Allegheny County roofs.
