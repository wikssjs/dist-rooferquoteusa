Reading the warranty on a Old Forge roof

There are two very different warranties on your new roof in Old Forge: the manufacturer’s warranty and the contractor’s workmanship warranty. It’s easy to confuse them, but they cover separate risks. Manufacturer warranties—often advertised as “lifetime”—actually cover material defects only, not leaks, and are almost always prorated after the first decade. If your shingles fail early because of a true defect, the manufacturer pays for new shingles (not labor) and typically expects photos, original receipts, and timely online registration, sometimes within 60 days.

The workmanship warranty, on the other hand, covers how the roof was installed. These run anywhere from a single year to 25 years in Old Forge, but remember—if a seven-person company set up shop two years ago, their 25-year warranty is only as good as their future in business. An established contractor with a 10-year track record offers more real security than a paper guarantee from a company nobody heard of last season.

Coverage is almost always voided if the roof was installed over old shingles (layering), or if there’s obvious poor ventilation—both are still seen in older Old Forge homes. Most warranties also state that improper attic ventilation, common here given our early-1900s homes, cancels coverage. Transferability at resale is usually limited or may require a transfer fee and paperwork within 30 days of the sale, so check the fine print before listing your home.

Decking, ventilation and flashing — the hidden roof costs

Most Old Forge roofing quotes are for what you can see: shingles or a membrane. But old wood-frame homes here, especially those built before 1980, hide surprises. Torn-up shingles often reveal rotted plank decking underneath—a very common discovery in the Lackawanna Valley’s older housing. If your roof deck needs new plywood, expect supplemental costs between $1,200–$3,000 depending on the size and extent.

Another frequent culprit is poor attic ventilation. Cold northeast Pennsylvania winters and a 40 psf snow load mean that heat escaping through an unvented attic will create ice dams, sending water under shingles and voiding the shingle warranty. Upgrading to ridge vent and modern soffit ventilation (often missing on Old Forge homes) typically adds $800–$1,600.

Chimney and wall flashing is not just “extra”—it’s the biggest source of roof leaks after the roof itself. Our older homes have more roof-to-wall transitions and masonry chimneys—properly reworking flashing, rather than reusing or “caulking it up,” usually adds $400–$1,200. Don’t forget vent boots (PVC stack covers), which crack and leak in hard winters and only cost $90–$250 each to replace. Always make sure drip edge and step flashing are specified in writing—these often get skipped in the race to offer a low bid, but they are code-required and can add a few hundred dollars to the total without breaking the budget.

Flat roof sections: the part most quotes get wrong

Nearly every Old Forge block has a mix of sloped main roofs and flat rear additions, porches, or rowhouse bump-outs. These flat sections need more than just shingles—shingles on a slope below 3/12 will fail fast here, especially with our persistent snow cover and winter freeze/thaw. Flat roofs need a continuous membrane, and in this market that means TPO or EPDM. These membranes can survive 15–25 years, about half the maximum life of a metal roof, but they’re built to withstand ponding water and rapid drainage cycles that destroy standard shingles.

It’s a red flag if a roofer quotes architectural shingles on a roof with a 2/12 pitch in Old Forge—a sign they either don’t know or don’t care about roof failures from ponding and leaks. If your rear porch or addition is leaking, expect to pay $11,500–$23,500 for a proper membrane replacement job. Unlike a simple repair, a full membrane replacement should also address details like wall flashing and mechanical penetrations. Don’t settle for patchwork: the right membrane matters, and so does the way it’s installed on these overlooked sections.

Filing a roof claim after a Pennsylvania storm

When Old Forge gets hit by a wind storm or heavy ice, the first step before any cleanup is to take clear photos of the damage—missing shingles, fallen branches, water stains inside. Insurance adjusters expect a complete “before” record, especially in Lackawanna County’s older wood-frame homes where one leak can quickly get lost in decades-old attic spaces.

Next, contact your insurer to start the claim and request a written scope of loss—the breakdown of what the adjuster sees as covered damage. In Pennsylvania, most homeowner policies pay either actual cash value (depreciated) or replacement cost (covers the total replacement, less your deductible). Read your coverage—older roofs may only be covered for actual cash value. Insurers usually send their own adjuster, but it’s wise to have your own contractor, familiar with Old Forge roofing, meet the adjuster and review any code-required upgrades.

By law under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA), your roofer must provide a detailed written contract for any roof job over $500 and cannot offer to “waive your deductible”—if someone tells you they’ll cover your out-of-pocket, that’s a red flag and a reason to say no. Pennsylvania enforces a three-business-day right of cancellation on all roof projects covered by HICPA. Always make sure written contracts spell out scope, which can be critical for complicated Old Forge roofs with hidden decking or ventilation issues.

Old Forge homes and their roof lines

Old Forge’s housing landscape is defined by stock from the early 1900s, with 79% of homes pre-dating 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). That means most roofs in town sit atop steeply pitched frames with plank sheathing—boards running horizontally or diagonally, not modern OSB or plywood. While these old decks can last, decades of weather, shifting, and ice damming leave gaps and rot that demand patching or entire new sheets during roof replacement.

Insulation and attic ventilation were afterthoughts in original construction. Most homes from this era lack modern ice-and-water barrier at the eaves—a must-have now due to the severe ridge-line winds and snow load in this valley. On top of that, older styles tended to allow two, sometimes even three, layers of shingles before a full tear-off. Tear-offs on these Old Forge houses often mean removing 30- or 40-year-old materials, plus an unknown amount of underlying wood.

Expect a new roof quote here to include not only shingle or membrane installation, but a contingency for plywood replacement and ventilation upgrades. The massive proportion of wooden row homes and duplexes drives frequent decking and flashing repairs, not just cosmetic reshingle jobs. Understanding what’s under your shingles is critical in Old Forge—old houses hide rot and lose heat fast, and lasting results mean opening up every layer back to the old boards before the new goes down.

Roofing permits in Old Forge: what you need before work starts

Roofing work in Old Forge falls under the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), based on the IRC. For any tear-off and full roof replacement on one- to four-family dwellings, you generally need a building permit; this is enforced by local municipalities, not handled county-wide. Like most places in this market, a simple repair—such as like-for-like shingle patches over a small area—typically does not require a permit in Old Forge or neighboring Dunmore.

If you’re in Scranton city limits, the City of Scranton Department of Licensing, Inspections & Permits issues and oversees permits and code compliance for roofing. For the rest of Lackawanna County, each municipality is responsible unless they’ve opted out (in which case the state Department of Labor & Industry steps in). Before hiring anyone, verify Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration through the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General at hic.attorneygeneral.gov, which is required for contracts over $500. Confirm workers’ compensation coverage using WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov)—this protects you if a worker is hurt on your property.

Written contracts are required by Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act for all jobs over $500, with a three-day right to cancel. No additional trade-specific roofing license exists here; if a contractor claims extra credentials or licensing for roofing, be wary. Genuine registration and current insurance are what matter in Old Forge.

Frequently asked questions — roofing in Old Forge

Does roof pitch affect the price in Old Forge? Roof pitch has a major impact on roofing price in Old Forge because most homes have steep slopes, especially in the early-1900s row and duplex stock dominating this area. A steeper pitch makes every step harder and adds extensive setup for safety, which increases labor hours. For example, replacing a standard architectural shingle roof on a steep Old Forge duplex can cost on the higher end of $9,300–$19,500 simply due to pitch and access challenges. Flat roof sections are priced separately, and if your roof includes a rear low-slope addition, expect additional costs for membrane material and flashings. Old houses with more angles and peaks require careful work on valleys and transitions, so always ask if your quote covers specialty labor for steep slopes—they are the rule here, not the exception.
How long does a roof replacement take in Old Forge? A full roof replacement in Old Forge usually takes between two and five days once work begins. The timeline is influenced by the size and complexity of the roof, the number of layers to tear off (older homes often have two or even three), and weather interruptions—which are common during Lackawanna County’s stormy springs and cold falls. For a typical single-family home needing architectural shingles, expect crews to finish within that window, but for metal or flat membrane roofs the work can take slightly longer due to custom flashing and detail work. Fast jobs may be possible for simple, small gable roofs, but any hidden decking rot or ventilation upgrades can add an extra day. On steep or cut-up roofs, labor is the limiting factor. Repair jobs—a few missing shingles or flashing fixes—are much quicker and often done same day for $475–$1,850.
Why is my Old Forge roofing quote higher than the online average? National “average roof replacement” numbers nearly always understate costs in Old Forge. Our pre-1980 homes yield hidden decking problems at tear-off, and most jobs here are more than just shingle swaps. Labor runs high because of steep roof lines, multi-layer tear-offs, and the need for custom flashing across rowhomes or additions. Material prices in northeast Pennsylvania also track regional supply—standing seam metal, for example, comes in at $22,500–$40,000 installed, more than you’ll see in a generic online chart. Finally, code-required upgrades, like proper attic ventilation or drip edge that may not have been present originally, are actually enforced here. Always judge your quote against Old Forge realities, not what a website in Florida or Texas says.
Are there historic district rules affecting roofing in Old Forge? Old Forge itself is not subject to the same historic district restrictions as some nearby cities; there is no special local board or registry imposing unique roofing material rules here according to available Lackawanna County documentation. However, zoning or architectural requirements could exist on a case-by-case basis, especially for highly visible properties or those in neighboring Scranton. Most Old Forge roofs can be replaced with modern materials, from architectural shingles $9,300–$19,500 to TPO/EPDM membranes $11,500–$23,500, without historic constraints. If you live in a property you suspect is historically significant, always check with your municipal office before work, but pure “historic review” is not the rule in Old Forge’s residential neighborhoods.
What is the difference between TPO and EPDM? Both TPO and EPDM are single-ply membranes used for flat or low-sloped roofs in Old Forge—a typical choice for rowhouse additions, porches, and commercial buildings. TPO (thermoplastic polyolefin) is white, which reflects sunlight and can lower attic temperatures. EPDM (ethylene propylene diene monomer) is black, better for snowy climates where snow melt is a plus. In terms of lifespan and price, both run about 15–25 years and are priced the same here—$11,500–$23,500 installed for most Old Forge homes. The real difference is installer preference and specific site needs; TPO tends to show seams less, while EPDM is excellent for complicated detail work. Either is far, far superior to putting shingles on a roof with less than a 3/12 pitch in this climate.
How long should a roof last in Old Forge? In Old Forge, architectural shingles (the market standard) typically last 25 to 30 years if installed with proper ventilation and underlayment. Class 4 impact-resistant shingles may offer a bit more durability, but our regional pattern of heavy snow, freeze-thaw cycles, and ice damming is hard on roofs. Membrane flat roofs, often on porches or additions, realistically last 15–25 years. Metal standing seam installations can run 40–70 years, but these are less common on historic rooflines. Actual lifespan here may be reduced by poor ventilation or the lack of ice and water shields on older houses. Replacement is a bigger investment—$9,300–$19,500 for shingles—but the better the initial install and attic ventilation, the closer you get to the upper range of longevity.
How do I file a storm damage roof claim in Pennsylvania? If your Old Forge roof is damaged in a storm, document everything: take photos of all visible damage, including missing shingles, holes, or leaks inside. Contact your insurer immediately to start a claim and ask for their adjuster to inspect the property. Get a written scope of loss and confirm whether your policy pays actual cash value or full replacement cost—a key difference for older roofs. Pick a local contractor who knows the ins and outs of old Scranton–Old Forge homes, and make sure they’re registered with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General. Never accept an offer to “waive your deductible”—that violates Pennsylvania law under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act and is grounds to walk away. If a leak can’t wait for adjuster inspection, emergency repair is allowed ($475–$1,850), but keep receipts and before/after documentation for your file.
Does Old Forge get enough hail to justify Class 4 shingles? Old Forge isn’t a hail hotspot like the Midwest, but we do get the occasional hailstorm—especially with fast-moving summer weather across the Lackawanna Valley ridges. Class 4 impact-resistant shingles aren’t essential for most homeowners, but if your house is in a particularly exposed spot or you want a potential homeowner’s insurance discount, they’re worth considering. In our market, a Class 4 shingle job typically runs $15,000–$19,500. Most insurance companies won’t require Class 4, but may reward it with premium savings. If you’ve had more than one hail event in recent seasons or are worried about branches and debris, upgrading brings peace of mind in storm season.
What should a roofing quote include in Old Forge? A solid Old Forge roofing quote should break out all materials by type and warranty, spell out how many layers are to be removed, specify whether decking repair is included or as-needed, and list the method of attic ventilation. Flashing, drip edge, ridge vent, and the cost for replacing rotted wood should be line items. For flat roof portions, look for membrane specification (TPO or EPDM), flashing details, and whether the quote covers any wall or chimney work. Pennsylvania law requires a detailed written contract for jobs over $500, so verbal promises aren’t enough—everything needs to be in writing. Review the quote for clear labor costs, warranty length, and payment schedule. If you see a single lump sum price without these details, get more answers before signing.
